Sprite test: a Blender lineman in Phaser

25 October 2026 · one test figure, four animations, eight facings, rendered on Jellybingus

It works, and the pipeline is cheap. A 3D figure rendered from a fixed camera reads clearly as a pixel sprite on the 60 px squares. Team colours come from tinting at runtime, so one set of renders covers every team. All 960 frames rendered in 56 seconds. The open questions are about taste, not feasibility: which pixel density you prefer, and how much modelling each race needs.

The model and the game today

How it was made

  1. scripts/sprites/blender_lineman.py runs inside Blender 5.1 on Jellybingus. It builds the figure, an orthographic camera 38° above the pitch, a sun and a toon material. The material steps the lighting into three flat bands.
  2. Poses are pure functions of animation and frame number, so there are no keyframes to manage. The script turns the figure through eight facings and renders each frame twice. The colour pass has the kit in grey. The mask pass marks primary kit red and secondary kit green.
  3. Anti-aliasing is off (filter size 0), so every pixel is a hard pixel straight from the renderer. There is no downscaling.
  4. scripts/sprites/build_sheets.py adds a 1 px dark outline. It then splits each frame into a base layer and two kit layers, and packs one sheet per animation with a row per facing.
  5. In Phaser each player is three stacked sprites on the same frame, with setTint() on the two kit layers. The origin sits on the feet anchor that Blender reports, so feet land on the square centre at any facing.

Render time: 960 frames (both sizes, both passes) in 56 s on the RTX 3090 with EEVEE. About 78% of each frame is empty, which is why trimming matters.

What the test showed

  • The look holds up. At the chunky density, the helmet, pads and kit colours read at a glance. The figure stands about 75 px tall on a 60 px square and overlaps the square behind it, the way a miniature does. Faces are a few pixels, so silhouette and kit carry identity, not features.
  • Tinting works. One set of renders covers every team. The grey kit keeps its three light bands under any tint, as the colour swap shows.
  • Eight facings fit Blood Bowl. A player can only move or block toward one of eight neighbouring squares, so eight facings cover every move with no in-between angles.
  • The camera angle is a compromise. The pitch is drawn straight down but the figures are seen from 38°. It looks fine to me, much like a top-down board with miniatures on it. Your eye is the real test.
  • Memory needs a packed atlas before scaling up. The raw sheets waste most of their pixels. Trimming the empty space, and replacing the two kit layers with one palette-swap shader, cuts a player type from 18 MB to about 1.4 MB at the chunky density.

What this test skipped

  • The figure is rigid primitives with procedural poses. Real players need sculpted, skinned meshes and keyframed animation, either by hand or retargeted from a motion library.
  • There is no ball-carrying, pass, catch, dodge or stand-up animation yet. The stand-up here is the fall played backwards.
  • Nothing touches the game code. PlayerSprite would swap its drawn shapes for these three sprites. The rings, markers and grid clicks would stay as they are.
  • The scale question stays open: 30 teams and 158 position images. The plan from the research note still applies: about eight to ten shared body rigs, with a shared animation set per rig and parts swapped per position.
